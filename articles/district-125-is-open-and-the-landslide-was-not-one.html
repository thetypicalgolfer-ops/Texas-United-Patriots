District 125 Is Open. The Landslide That Wasn't.

September 12, 2026 | 4 min read | Texas United Patriots Staff
Spread the Word

For the first time in years there is no incumbent on the ballot in Texas House District 125. The number everyone reported out of the Democratic runoff was eighty percent. That number is real. It is also the most misleading figure in this race.

Start with what actually changed. State Representative Ray Lopez is not seeking reelection. That single decision turned District 125 from a defense into an open seat, and an open seat is a different contest with different math. Nobody's name is already on the office. Nobody starts with the advantage of having been there.

The district runs through parts of West and Northwest San Antonio, out through Leon Valley, to the edge of Government Canyon State Natural Area. If you live in it, the seat that represents you in Austin is genuinely up for grabs this November.

The Eighty Percent

On May 26, the Democratic runoff ended 80.1 percent to 19.9 percent. Every write-up used the word landslide, and on that day, against that opponent, it was one.

Now put it next to the number from eleven weeks earlier.

In the March 3 primary, with the full field on the ballot, the same eventual winner finished first with just over 39 percent. The runner-up took about 34 percent. Nobody cleared a majority — which is the only reason there was a runoff at all.

So in the larger election, six out of ten Democratic primary voters picked somebody else. The eighty percent came later, in a May runoff — and a primary runoff is the smallest, lowest-turnout election on the American calendar. It is the contest almost nobody shows up for.

Both numbers are true. They describe different electorates. The one that got reported is the one drawn from the smallest room.

And November Is A Third Electorate

Here is the part that matters more than either figure: a Democratic primary is not a general election. Only voters who chose to participate in one party's nominating contest were in those rooms. In November the door opens to everyone in the district — Republicans, Democrats, and the large number of people who skip primaries entirely and turn out for the general.

Nobody has measured that group yet this cycle. Not in March, not in May. A percentage won in a closed nominating contest tells you who a party picked. It does not tell you what the district will do.

The Dates That Decide It

This is the part worth writing down, because every argument above is academic if these pass you by.

Monday, October 5 is the last day to register to vote for this election. If you moved, if you turned eighteen, if you have never done it — that is the deadline, and it is roughly three weeks out.

Early voting runs Monday, October 19 through Friday, October 30. Twelve days. In Texas you may vote early at any early-voting location in your county, not just the one nearest your house.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3. Polls are open 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. If you are in line at 7 p.m., you vote.

Requesting a mail ballot has its own deadline — eleven days before the election — and a marked mail ballot has to be in the hands of your election clerk by 7 p.m. on Election Day. Late is not counted. You can check your registration status at the Texas Secretary of State's own site, VoteTexas.gov.

The Real Headline

An eighty-percent runoff win is a headline. An open seat is a fact. The gap between them is the thing nobody is going to put in a push notification.

Districts like this one do not change hands because somebody gave a better speech. They change hands because a few thousand people who were not counted in March or May decide to show up in November — or because they don't.

October 5 to register. October 19 through 30 to vote early. November 3 by 7 p.m. Everything else in this article is commentary.

Don't Get Counted Out

October 5 is the registration deadline. Check your status at VoteTexas.gov, then tell one neighbour in District 125 who thinks their vote doesn't move anything.
